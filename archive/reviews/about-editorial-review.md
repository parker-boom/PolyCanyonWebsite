## Proposed public text

### What is Poly Canyon?

Poly Canyon is a valley of trails, grassland, and student-built structures in the hills northeast of Cal Poly’s campus. Within the wider canyon, a nine-acre outdoor construction laboratory gives architecture and engineering students room to build at full scale. Its bridges, houses, towers, and small structural experiments have accumulated over more than sixty years.

The site exists because some things are difficult to learn from a drawing or model alone. Students have used it to test how materials behave, how a structure carries weight, and how a design becomes something people can use. Projects have involved finding materials, working with faculty and tradespeople, building on uneven ground, and adapting when the original idea did not work as expected. Some took several student teams and years to finish.

For a visitor, those experiments are scattered through an open hillside rather than arranged in a gallery. Paths connect concrete shells, exposed steel frames, timber structures, and bridges over the seasonal creek. Some structures are still used for teaching; others have been repaired, altered, or partly lost. The name Architecture Graveyard reflects the weathered condition of many projects, but the canyon also remains a place for new student construction, outdoor visits, and the annual Design Village competition.

### Visiting

Poly Canyon is open to the public year-round. A daylight visit is recommended for seeing the structures and finding your way along the paths. Follow any posted restrictions or temporary closures.

Start at the H-4f parking lot on the northeast side of Cal Poly’s campus. Follow Poly Canyon Road through the yellow gate and continue along the road to the Entry Arch, which marks the entrance to the structures.

Bring water and wear shoes for uneven ground. Give wildlife and horses space, and expect muddy paths after rain.

### History

#### Finding room to build: the 1950s and 1960s

Before the structures, the canyon was agricultural land associated with Peterson Ranch and Cal Poly. As the architectural engineering program grew after the Second World War, students needed somewhere to carry out experiments too large for a classroom or workshop. George Hasslein, who later became the college’s first dean, helped establish the canyon as that space.

The laboratory took shape in the early 1960s. Students moved the Geodesic Dome from campus and began projects intended to test construction methods at full size. Building the site also meant providing water, electricity, and ways across the creek. Houses such as the Modular House developed over several years as successive teams added to earlier work. The canyon became both a collection of experiments and a place equipped to support more building.

#### Living and working in the canyon: the 1970s and 1980s

Resident caretakers became part of that arrangement in the early 1970s. Glen Jackson was the first resident, and later students lived in several of the canyon’s houses. In exchange for housing and a meal plan, caretakers maintained the grounds and helped keep the site ready for studio work and events. Daily upkeep was part of what allowed an exposed outdoor laboratory to function.

During the 1970s and 1980s, students added places to gather, work, and study alongside experiments with concrete, timber, earth, and passive solar design. The houses were lived in and modified, so a project could continue changing after its original builders graduated.

Design Village added a different kind of building cycle. The architecture department’s 2010 history dates its start to a student proposal in 1974: teams would design shelters, build them in the canyon, and live in them during Open House. The temporary village brought students from other schools into the site and became a recurring event alongside the permanent projects.

#### Maintaining an established site: the 1990s and 2000s

Construction continued at an uneven pace as the early structures aged. New work, including the Tensile Structure, joined projects that already needed repair. Caretakers continued looking after the site, and Design Village returned each spring.

Restoration also became a student project in its own right. Rebuilding the Blade in 2003 required a new team to revisit the original calculations and work with returning alumni; the Stick Structure was rebuilt in 2009 using materials chosen to reduce maintenance. Students were now learning from the long-term performance of earlier experiments as well as adding their own.

#### The end of resident care and later repairs: the 2010s

The residential caretaker program ended around 2011. The loss of daily on-site care changed how the canyon was maintained, while weathering and vandalism continued to affect the structures. It was a substantial change for buildings that had served as homes and working spaces.

Students organized the Canyon Days Committee in 2014 to help preserve the site. Its work included cleanup, graffiti removal, path maintenance, and repairs. Larger rehabilitation projects followed. In 2017 and 2018, student teams converted the deteriorating Modular House into an open observation structure, replacing damaged elements and adding stairs and guardrails. Restoration gave an older building a different use rather than recreating its former life as a residence.

#### New construction and continued care: the late 2010s and 2020s

Recent work includes repairs to Bridge House and Cantilever Deck, along with new projects such as Observation Deck, Tensegrity, and the 2024 Moment Monument. These have required student teams to take designs through engineering review, fundraising, material sourcing, and construction. They continue the original purpose of the laboratory while responding to the practical demands of an aging site.

Design Village continues to bring temporary shelters into the canyon, and student maintenance efforts have renewed alongside the capstone projects. The result is a site containing several periods of work at once: original structures, surviving fragments, later repairs, and new designs. Both building and caring for what is already there remain part of its history.

### About this archive

Parker Jones began this project as a Cal Poly student by mapping the canyon’s paths and structures from aerial photography. He developed the iPhone app to share that map, then gathered research with help from Kennedy Library and students in the College of Architecture and Environmental Design.

This website collects structure descriptions, photographs, and original project reports. Records are uneven and may show earlier conditions. Individual pages link to the available sources, including materials compiled by Danny Wills’s architecture studio, Jesse Vestermark’s library research guide, and CAED.
